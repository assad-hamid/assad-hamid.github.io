Trajectory-Based Analysis of Pedestrian–Vehicle Conflict Severity

Dalian University of Technology · Transportation Planning and Management · Research in progress

How does the severity of a pedestrian–vehicle encounter become distinguishable before the conflict is realized—and what part of that distinction is carried by the interacting pair rather than the surrounding traffic?

Research focus

Pedestrian–vehicle safety at unsignalized intersections is often evaluated through crash records or event-level surrogate measures. These indicators are useful for identifying critical encounters, but provide a limited account of how risk-related motion develops. This research examines conflict severity as a trajectory-based phenomenon, bringing together predictive modelling, comparable-case analysis and the study of observable road-user responses.

Naturalistic drone trajectories from four unsignalized intersections in Germany, drawn from the inD and DeepScenario Open 3D datasets, are harmonized into a common spatial and temporal representation. The fundamental unit of analysis is a pedestrian–vehicle interaction rather than an individual track. Interactions are located around their shared conflict geometry and characterized with complementary surrogate safety measures—post-encroachment time (PET) and minimum time-to-collision (mTTC)—to distinguish critical from normal encounters.

Analytical approach

With a common event catalogue established, the investigation first asks where useful predictive information resides. A controlled set of models progressively considers focal-pair motion summaries, nonlinear relationships, ordered trajectory sequences and representations of the wider traffic scene. These alternatives are compared on consistent targets and recording-separated evaluation data, with attention to class imbalance and the risk of incorporating future information into prediction. This design distinguishes improvements attributable to accessible information from changes caused merely by model complexity.

Prediction alone cannot establish when critical and normal encounters start to differ. The analysis therefore proceeds to a matched case–control comparison, pairing critical interactions with normal encounters having comparable measured approach conditions. A multivariate matching design considers interaction geometry, vehicle approach speed and relative arrival timing, while remaining differences in entry timing are carried forward for statistical adjustment. The matched encounters are subsequently aligned to a common conflict-relative time reference.

Spline-based linear mixed-effects models are applied to the resulting case–control difference trajectories to examine nonlinear changes in vehicle motion, pedestrian motion and the surrounding traffic context. The models accommodate repeated temporal observations; bootstrap-based simultaneous inference and multiplicity-aware comparisons assess which differences are supported across the observation window, rather than at isolated frames. Reserved matched encounters and sensitivity analyses provide complementary checks on the resulting temporal interpretation.

Finally, a severity-stratified behavioural assessment returns to the broader interaction catalogue. Vehicle approach speed, deceleration, yielding and evasive manoeuvres are examined alongside pedestrian speed and directional responses. This places the time-resolved findings within a more interpretable behavioural account of how road users respond under differing levels of conflict severity.

Emerging evidence

The predictive comparisons indicate that nonlinear relationships within the focal pedestrian–vehicle pair contain substantial severity-related information; more elaborate temporal and scene representations do not demonstrate a correspondingly reliable incremental improvement under the evaluated conditions. This finding concerns the tested representations and data, not the absence of any possible contextual influence.

The matched temporal analysis identifies an ordered kinematic pattern: more critical encounters exhibit greater vehicle approach speed and faster pair convergence within the observable pre-conflict interval, while a comparatively stronger deceleration difference appears later. The measured surrounding-context variables do not show an independently supported temporal signature under the specified analysis. Complementary behavioural comparisons associate more severe interactions particularly with vehicle-side motion and response characteristics; pedestrian-side distinctions are less uniform.

Research contribution and scope

The work links three questions that are often treated separately: what predicts interaction severity, when comparable encounters become distinguishable, and how that distinction is expressed in road-user behaviour. Its contribution is an integrated analytical perspective that moves beyond event classification toward a statistically qualified account of conflict development, without assuming that more complex models necessarily provide more usable safety information.

This is an overview of ongoing, unpublished Master’s research. Reported interpretations are observational and specific to the studied intersections, trajectory coverage and implemented measures; they should not be read as causal estimates, prospective collision-warning performance or evidence of general applicability across all traffic settings.
